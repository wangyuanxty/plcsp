\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Shop configuration}

We consider a job shop with $J$ jobs and $M$ machines. Each job $j$ consists
of $O$ operations that must be processed in a fixed order. Operation $o$ of
job $j$ can be assigned to a subset
$\mathcal{M}_{j,o}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,M\}$ of machines. If it is assigned to
machine $m$, its processing time is $t_{j,o,m}>0$ and its processing power is
$p_{j,o,m}>0$. The processing energy is
\begin{equation}
e_{j,o,m}=t_{j,o,m}\,p_{j,o,m}.
\end{equation}

The machines are partitioned into $W$ workshops. Workshop $w$ owns a fleet
$\mathcal{A}_w$ of automated guided vehicles (AGVs). An AGV remains in its
home workshop for the whole episode and carries one load at a time.

\subsection{Transport}

Let $m$ and $m'$ be the machines of two consecutive operations of the same
job, and let $u_w$ be the load/unload station of workshop $w$. A job enters
the shop at the station of the workshop that hosts its first operation, so
the first operation is moved from $u_w$ to its machine. If $m=m'$, no
transport is required.

Let $d_{a,b}$ be the travel time between two nodes, where a node is either a
machine or a station. A machine belongs to exactly one workshop, and
$d_{m,u_w}$ is the distance from machine $m$ to its own station.

If $m$ and $m'$ belong to the same workshop, one AGV of that workshop
performs the transport. The AGV first travels empty from its current position
to $m$, and then carries the job to $m'$. The loaded travel time is
$d_{m,m'}$; the empty travel time is $d_{p,m}$, where $p$ is the position of
the AGV.

If $m$ and $m'$ belong to different workshops, the transport uses the two
stations:
\begin{equation}
m \;\xrightarrow{\text{AGV of }w}\; u_{w}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{transfer}}\; u_{w'}
\;\xrightarrow{\text{AGV of }w'}\; m',
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $w$ and $w'$ are the workshops of $m$ and $m'$. The transfer between
the two stations consumes no AGV. In the experiments the machine-to-machine
time $d_{m,m'}$ is cut into three parts: a source leg and a target leg that
share the remaining time in equal halves, and a fixed transfer delay of five
time units between them that consumes no AGV. A
source-workshop AGV performs the first leg and stays at $u_w$ afterwards; a
target-workshop AGV performs the last leg.

The transport term of the objective counts both the loaded and the empty
AGV travel. The empty travel is charged at a fraction $\rho_e$ of the loaded
power, $\rho_e=0.5$ in the experiments.

\subsection{Objectives}

Let $C_{j,o}$ be the completion time of operation $(j,o)$ and let $S_{j,o}$
be its start time. The makespan is
\begin{equation}
\Cmax=\max_{j,o} C_{j,o}.
\end{equation}
The mean processing energy is
\begin{equation}
\Eproc=\frac{1}{JO}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{o=1}^{O} e_{j,o,m(j,o)},
\end{equation}
where $m(j,o)$ is the machine assigned to operation $(j,o)$.

Let $\mathcal{O}_m$ be the set of operations assigned to machine $m$. The
idle energy is
\begin{equation}
\Eidle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(
\Cmax-\sum_{(j,o)\in\mathcal{O}_m} t_{j,o,m}\right).
\label{eq:idle}
\end{equation}
Every machine is charged from time zero to the makespan, so the term counts
the gaps before and between its operations and the tail after the last one.
Idle power is constant and normalized to one, so \Eidle{} equals the total
idle time; the two terms are used interchangeably. The reading differs from
the sum of the gaps alone, which stops at the last operation on each machine;
Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states which reading the baselines use.

Let $\Ttrans$ be the loaded travel time plus $\rho_e$ times the empty travel
time, summed over all transports. The
scalar objective is
\begin{equation}
F=w_{1}\Cmax+w_{2}\left(\Eproc+\Eidle\right)+w_{3}\Ttrans,
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with $w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}=1$. We evaluate with $(w_1,w_2,w_3)=(0.4,0.4,0.2)$.
During training the weights are sampled at random and are fixed within a
group of rollouts (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

The three terms have different scales. Makespan and transport time are in
time units, and processing and idle energy are in energy units. The weights
set the trade-off. Idle energy, by contrast, depends on the assignment and the
sequence and not only on the processing times: moving an operation to a machine
with a later release can reduce its processing energy and increase the idle
energy of the machine it leaves.

\subsection{Assumptions}

We make the following assumptions.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(A1)] AGVs are assigned to workshops and do not move between
workshops.
\item[(A2)] Each workshop has one load/unload station. A job enters the
shop at the station of the workshop that hosts its first operation, and
a cross-workshop transport goes from the source machine to the source
station, transfers between the two stations, and continues from the
target station to the target machine.
\item[(A3)] AGVs are single-load. Loading and unloading take zero time.
There is no charging and no AGV failure.
\item[(A4)] The transport term of \eqref{eq:objective} counts loaded and
empty AGV travel; empty travel is charged at $\rho_e=0.5$ of the loaded
power. AGV waiting is not charged.
\item[(A5)] The position of every AGV is tracked at all times, including
a source AGV that has stopped at its station.
\end{itemize}

Assumptions (A1)--(A2) define the transport model. Assumptions (A3)--(A5)
are scope limits; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states their effect on the
reported numbers.

\subsection{Decision process}

The schedule is constructed in $JO$ low-level steps. At step $t$, the state
$s_t$ contains the partial disjunctive graph, the machine routes, the AGV
states, and the current weights. The action is a triple
$a_t=(a^{\mathrm{job}}_t,a^{\mathrm{mach}}_t,a^{\mathrm{agv}}_t)$. The
operation is chosen from the unscheduled operations whose predecessor is
complete, the machine from the eligible machines of that operation, and the
AGV from the AGVs of the source workshop when a transport is needed, or from
the target workshop when the transport crosses a workshop boundary.

A transition applies the assignment, inserts the operation into the machine
route, updates the AGV states, and returns the next state. One episode ends
after $JO$ steps, and the return is the negative objective
\eqref{eq:objective} under the weights of the episode.

At step $t$ the number of joint actions is
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{A}_t|=
|\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{feas}}_t|\times
|\mathcal{M}_{j,o}|\times
|\mathcal{A}_{w}|,
\end{equation}
where the last factor is present only when a transport is needed. It grows
with the number of jobs, machines, and AGVs. A policy must rank
the candidates without enumerating the product, which motivates the
factorized policy in \eqref{eq:factor}.

\subsection{Complexity}

The problem contains the flexible job shop as a special case (one workshop,
one AGV), so it is NP-hard. Its joint action space is the product of the
three candidate sets, and the number of feasible schedules grows with the number
of machine assignments and with the number of AGV sequences. An exact model
must decide the assignment, the machine sequence, and the vehicle sequence
together, so its size grows with all three.

Two further couplings enter through the objective. The idle term couples the
assignment with the sequence: an operation that starts earlier on one machine
can leave a larger gap on another. By the same argument, the transport term
couples the machine sequence with the vehicle sequence, because a shorter
loaded trip can require a longer empty trip. These couplings are why a fixed
rule pair is not enough and why the controller learns the assignment, the
sequence, and the vehicle choice together.
