\section{Problem Definition}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Shop configuration}

We consider a job shop with $J$ jobs and $M$ machines. Each job $j$ consists
of $O$ operations that must be processed in a fixed order. Operation $o$ of
job $j$ can be assigned to a subset
$\mathcal{M}_{j,o}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,M\}$ of machines. If it is assigned to
machine $m$, its processing time is $t_{j,o,m}>0$ and its processing power is
$p_{j,o,m}>0$. The processing energy is
\begin{equation}
e_{j,o,m}=t_{j,o,m}\,p_{j,o,m}.
\end{equation}

The machines are partitioned into $W$ workshops. Workshop $w$ owns a fleet
$\mathcal{A}_w$ of automated guided vehicles (AGVs). An AGV remains in its
home workshop for the whole episode and carries one load at a time.

\subsection{Transport}

Let $m$ and $m'$ be the machines of two consecutive operations of the same
job. If $m=m'$, no transport is required. Otherwise the job must be moved
from $m$ to $m'$. Let $d_{m,m'}$ be the travel time between the two machines.

If $m$ and $m'$ belong to the same workshop, one AGV of that workshop
performs the transport. The loaded travel time is $d_{m,m'}$. The AGV first
travels empty from its current position to $m$. The empty travel time is
added to the start time of the next operation, but not to the transport time
in the objective.

If $m$ and $m'$ belong to different workshops, the transport is split into
two legs with a handover delay:
\begin{equation}
d_{m,m'}=\tau^{s}_{m,m'}+\delta_{m,m'}+\tau^{d}_{m,m'},
\label{eq:split}
\end{equation}
where $\delta_{m,m'}=\min(\delta,d_{m,m'})$ is a fixed delay that consumes
no AGV, and $\tau^{s}$ and $\tau^{d}$ are the loaded travel times of the
source and target legs. The remaining travel $d_{m,m'}-\delta_{m,m'}$ is
split between the two legs by a fixed ratio $\rho$. A source-workshop AGV
performs the first leg and a target-workshop AGV performs the second leg.
The experiments use $\delta=5$ and $\rho=0.5$.

\subsection{Objectives}

Let $C_{j,o}$ be the completion time of operation $(j,o)$ and let $S_{j,o}$
be its start time. The makespan is
\begin{equation}
\Cmax=\max_{j,o} C_{j,o}.
\end{equation}
The mean processing energy is
\begin{equation}
\Eproc=\frac{1}{JO}\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{o=1}^{O} e_{j,o,m(j,o)},
\end{equation}
where $m(j,o)$ is the machine assigned to operation $(j,o)$.

Let $\mathcal{O}_m$ be the set of operations assigned to machine $m$. The
idle energy is
\begin{equation}
\Eidle=\sum_{m=1}^{M}\left(
\max_{(j,o)\in\mathcal{O}_m} C_{j,o}
-\sum_{(j,o)\in\mathcal{O}_m} t_{j,o,m}\right).
\label{eq:idle}
\end{equation}
The expression equals the sum of the initial idle interval and all gaps on
every machine. To see this, order the operations on machine $m$ by start
time. The initial interval is $S_{m,1}$. Each gap is
$S_{m,i+1}-C_{m,i}$. The sum telescopes to $C_{m,\mathrm{last}}-
\sum_i t_{m,i}$, which is \eqref{eq:idle}. Idle power is constant and
normalized to one, so \Eidle{} equals the total idle time; the two terms are
used interchangeably.

Let $\Ttrans$ be the sum of loaded travel times over all transports. The
scalar objective is
\begin{equation}
F=w_{1}\Cmax+w_{2}\left(\Eproc+\Eidle\right)+w_{3}\Ttrans,
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
with $w_{1}+w_{2}+w_{3}=1$. The evaluation uses $(w_1,w_2,w_3)=(0.4,0.4,0.2)$.
During training the weights are sampled at random and are fixed within a
group of rollouts (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

The three terms have different scales. The makespan and the transport time
are in time units; the processing and idle energy are in energy units. The
weights set the trade-off. The idle term depends on the assignment and the
sequence, not only on the processing times: moving an operation to a machine
with a later release can reduce its processing energy and increase the idle
energy of the machine it leaves.

\subsection{Assumptions}

The model makes the following assumptions.
\begin{itemize}
\item[(A1)] AGVs are assigned to workshops and do not move between
workshops.
\item[(A2)] A cross-workshop transport consists of a source leg, a fixed
handover delay, and a target leg. The handover point is the first machine of
the target workshop.
\item[(A3)] AGVs are single-load. Loading and unloading take zero time.
There is no charging and no AGV failure.
\item[(A4)] The transport time in \eqref{eq:objective} counts loaded travel
only. Empty travel and waiting are part of the schedule but not of
$\Ttrans$.
\item[(A5)] After a source leg, the position of the source AGV is not
tracked; its next empty travel is approximated as zero.
\end{itemize}

Assumptions (A1)--(A2) define the transport model. Assumptions (A3)--(A5)
are scope limits; Section~\ref{sec:experiments} states their effect on the
reported numbers.

\subsection{Decision process}

The schedule is constructed in $JO$ low-level steps. At step $t$, the state
$s_t$ contains the partial disjunctive graph, the machine routes, the AGV
states, and the current weights. The action is a triple
$a_t=(a^{\mathrm{job}}_t,a^{\mathrm{mach}}_t,a^{\mathrm{agv}}_t)$. The
operation is chosen from the unscheduled operations whose predecessor is
complete. The machine is chosen from the eligible machines of that
operation. The AGV is chosen from the AGVs of the source workshop when a
transport is needed, and from the target workshop when the transport crosses
a workshop boundary.

The transition applies the assignment, inserts the operation into the
machine route, updates the AGV states, and returns the next state. The
episode ends after $JO$ steps. The return is the negative objective
\eqref{eq:objective} under the weights of the episode.

The number of joint actions at step $t$ is
\begin{equation}
|\mathcal{A}_t|=
|\mathcal{O}^{\mathrm{feas}}_t|\times
|\mathcal{M}_{j,o}|\times
|\mathcal{A}_{w}|,
\end{equation}
where the last factor is present only when a transport is needed. The
product grows with the number of jobs, machines, and AGVs. A policy must rank
the candidates without enumerating the product, which motivates the
factorized policy in \eqref{eq:factor}.

\subsection{Complexity}

The problem contains the flexible job shop as a special case (one workshop,
one AGV), so it is NP-hard. The joint action space is the product of the
three candidate sets. The number of feasible schedules grows with the number
of machine assignments and with the number of AGV sequences. An exact model
must decide the assignment, the machine sequence, and the vehicle sequence
together; the mixed-integer baseline in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} solves
the first two and the third in two stages.

The objective adds two sources of coupling. The idle term couples the
assignment with the sequence: an operation that starts earlier on one machine
can leave a larger gap on another. The transport term couples the machine
sequence with the vehicle sequence: a shorter loaded trip can require a
longer empty trip. These couplings are why a fixed rule pair is not enough
and why the controller learns the assignment, the sequence, and the vehicle
choice together.
