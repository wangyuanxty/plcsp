\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This study proposes a hierarchical multi-agent model for joint production and
logistics scheduling in a flexible job shop with several workshops. Four
agents carry it: a rule selector sets a rule prior every few steps, and three
shop agents make the operation, machine, and AGV decisions; each shop agent
encodes its own candidates with a Set Transformer; the shop agents are trained with a shared
group-relative advantage without critic.

Our experiments show that the encoder and the multi-actor update each improve
over their respective baselines, and that our model is fast enough for
interactive use. Its objective is lower than that of the fixed dispatching
rules and of the adapted DRL baselines at every size. Against RGA the outcome
depends on the size. At $6\times6$ RGA returns a 12.2\%
lower objective; at $10\times6$ the paired test does not separate our model from RGA; at the largest size our model is 20.1\% ahead. That
crossover, search time in the small shop against a single pass in the large
one, is the operating point of the method. One repair in the middle of the
schedule separates the two further: our model loses less than the search
does, because it can take another machine, and it ends with the lowest
objective of the three. The weights are inputs, so one policy serves nine
weight points without retraining. At $6\times6$ its gap to the search stays
between 10 and 19 per cent at eight of them and widens to 89 per cent when only
transport counts; at $10\times10$ it is ahead of the search at all nine, by
11.6 to 30.6 per cent. Rule
selection has a small and uneven effect. Rule-usage analysis shows that the
selector is
not a fixed rule: as the partial schedule fills, the machine rule head moves
from minimum processing time to minimum ready time, the AGV rule head from
earliest arrival to nearest, and the job rule head from least work remaining
to earliest finish. At $10\times6$ the selector's mean effect falls to 1.3\%,
while the learned AGV head is still better than the best fixed rule by
7.3\%.

Three questions frame the practice: how the decisions are split, how the
policy is trained, and when the schedule is rebuilt. On the first, the three
decisions are coupled, so a planner cannot treat vehicle dispatching as a
separate subproblem; a schedule that improves machine utilization alone leaves
either machines waiting for vehicles or vehicles running empty. On the second,
the three shop agents share one team return from the plant objective, so no
reward has to be defined for each agent and no value critic is needed; reward
design appears only at the top level, where each rule head receives the change
of its own local cost. Because the weights enter the state, one policy covers a
range of priorities without retraining. On the third, the choice between search
and a learned policy follows the scale of the shop and how often the schedule is
rebuilt: search pays off in a small shop with a one-off plan, while the policy
pays off in a large shop, or when disruptions and priority changes force
frequent re-scheduling. The three answers support each other: the joint model
gives the policy the full state, the shared return removes per-agent critics,
and a low re-scheduling cost makes frequent updates practical.

Meanwhile, the extension of the joint scheduling framework to cross-enterprise
supply chain coordination and its integration with organizational
decision-making processes will be explored to further enhance the managerial
decision-making value of the proposed dynamic scheduling method.
