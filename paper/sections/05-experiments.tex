\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

We use three instance families: $6\times6\times2$ (six jobs, six machines,
two workshops), $10\times6\times2$, and $10\times10\times2$. Each family has
100 test instances. All
methods are evaluated on the same instances, with the weights fixed to
$(0.4,0.4,0.2)$ in \eqref{eq:objective}. The learned policies are evaluated
greedily. Each learned method is trained with three seeds; the reported value
is the mean of the three seed-level test means. The rule-based and
metaheuristic baselines are deterministic given their own seed.

The training budget is 200 episodes for every learned method. The group size
is 8, the instance batch is resampled every 8 episodes, and the learning rate
is $10^{-3}$ with Adam and no decay. These settings are identical across the
ablations; only the factor under test changes.

Instances are generated with the generator of Wang
et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}; the parameter ranges are theirs. Each
job has $M$ operations, and each
operation is eligible on a subset of machines. The mean processing time is
drawn from $U(1,99)$ and the mean processing power from $U(1,20)$; the value
on a given machine is the mean multiplied by a factor from $U(0.8,1.2)$.
Same-workshop travel times are drawn from $U(1,10)$ and cross-workshop travel
times from $U(10,20)$. The machines are split equally between the two
workshops, and each workshop has three AGVs. The training pool contains
12{,}800 instances (seed 0), the validation set 100 instances (seed 1), and
the test set 100 instances (seed 3); the three pools are disjoint. A
checkpoint is selected on the validation set, and the test set is used once
after training.

The objective of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses the same four
terms. They set the standby power of the machines and the transport power of
the vehicles to one, so their energy terms equal the idle time and the loaded
transport time used here. The transport model differs: they assume that the
transport machines of a workshop are adequate, whereas here each workshop has
three AGVs and a cross-workshop trip includes a station transfer. The absolute
objective values in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} are therefore not directly
comparable with those reported in that paper.

\subsection{Baselines}

The baselines are adaptations of published methods, not reproductions. Two of the papers release no code; those baselines are written from the paper and are labelled as adapted below. Every baseline is evaluated on the same instances and the same objective as the controller.

\paragraph{Rule-based} Twelve dispatching rules combine six job rules (FIFO,
MOR, and the most- and least-work-remaining forms on processing time and on
processing energy) with two machine rules (shortest processing time and
minimum processing energy), as in Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}. AGVs follow the earliest-free rule. The best
rule pair is reported.

\paragraph{RGA} A random-key genetic algorithm with population 50 and 100
generations, i.e.\ 4850 schedule evaluations per instance, with the operators
of Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2011rga}. It optimises the operation sequence and
the machine assignment; AGVs are dispatched by the earliest-free rule. The
objective values are computed with a direct decoder, which reproduces the four
simulator cost components exactly on random schedules; for the run-time
measurement, each of the 4850 evaluations is replayed through the same DG
simulator as the controller.

\paragraph{MIP (two-stage CP-SAT)} Stage~A solves the
machine assignment and the operation start times with a CP-SAT model. Stage~B
routes the AGVs with a second CP-SAT model that assigns each transport leg to
a workshop fleet and sequences the legs of each AGV; Stage~B minimises a
routing proxy, the sum of the final-leg completion times. The resulting
schedule is re-simulated and scored with \eqref{eq:objective}, and the best of
three alternations is kept. The idle term \eqref{eq:idle} and the station
transfer \eqref{eq:split} follow the simulator. The time limit is 10~s for
Stage~A and 8~s for each Stage~B solve; at $10\times10$, half the instances
need a 60~s routing limit.

\paragraph{B3} The multi-action DRL framework of Lei et
al.~\cite{lei2022} is evaluated with the checkpoint released for each instance
size: the job and machine actors are used greedily, the AGV is dispatched by
the earliest-free rule, and the machine input is zero-padded to our feature
width. It is a single checkpoint per size, not a three-seed method. We report
it as an adapted baseline, not as a reproduction.

\paragraph{E2E} The architecture of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses a GIN job encoder and a GAT machine
encoder. We train it with PPO under the same protocol, with the earliest-free
AGV rule. It is the published architecture retrained, not a reproduction of
the numbers in that paper.

\paragraph{Li et al.} The multi-agent method of Li et
al.~\cite{li2025plcsp} is adapted. Four actors (job filter, job selection,
machine selection, and AGV selection) each have their own critic and output
small rule-weight or proto-action vectors; a hand-written priority vector
makes the choice. The original problem has fixed machines and total weighted
tardiness; here the actors choose from the eligible machines and the
objective is \eqref{eq:objective}, and the due-date rules are replaced by
energy and transport rules. It is trained for 1500 episodes, the budget of
the original paper. The authors released no code; the baseline is written
from the paper and is not a reproduction.

\paragraph{Shi et al.} The nested method of Shi et al.~\cite{shi2025nested}
is adapted. An operation agent, a process agent with nine composite rules,
and an AGV agent act in sequence; each is trained with its own PPO update on
a shared reward that splits the makespan from the remaining objective. The
original problem models AGV failures and load/unload stations; here the
instances are static and the stations are not modelled. It is trained for
1000 episodes. This baseline is also written from the paper.

\paragraph{Learned baselines} PPO with per-head critics replaces GRPO in the
same architecture. The no-RS variant removes the top-level rule selector.

\subsection{Comparison under the protocol of Wang et al.}

Table~\ref{tab:e2eproto} removes the AGV fleet and returns to the transport
model of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}: travel is machine to machine,
it is charged when the two consecutive operations use different machines, and
only loaded travel is counted. Under this protocol the instance generation and
the objective are those of that paper, so the two studies can be compared.

The controller obtains 559.31 at $6\times6$ and 575.27 at $10\times6$. The
all-GNN encoder, which is the architecture of that paper trained under the
same protocol, obtains 676.99 and 678.88. The published P-G model obtains
732.5 and 674.5, and the published rule pair 1045.6 and 1288.2. The controller
is therefore 23.6\% below P-G at $6\times6$ and 14.7\% below it at
$10\times6$, 36.0\% and 31.4\% below the published DRL baseline, and 46.5\%
and 55.3\% below the best published rule pair.

One caveat applies. The decoder used here packs operations into machine gaps,
whereas the environment of that paper disables this shift for its rules. The
same rule pair obtains 913.87 here against 1045.6 there, so 12.6\% of the gap
comes from the decoder and not from the policy. The comparison supports the
relative ordering within each study; it does not support a claim about
absolute differences.

\subsection{Scale test at $10\times6$}

At $10\times6$ the full model obtains 576.92, against 575.27 for the same
controller without the selector and the AGV head. The difference is 1.65,
smaller than the seed range of the full model (11.33), so the study is
underpowered to separate the two configurations at this size. The controller
beats PDR by 524.38 and B3 by 74.38, and is 11.29 behind RGA.

\label{sec:ablations}

559.31, the job-GNN/machine-ST cell 580.21, the job-ST/machine-GNN cell

\paragraph{Rule usage}
The mean probability of each rule at the five top-level decision points, over
20 test instances (seed 1), shows that the selector is not a fixed rule. The
machine head moves from minimum energy (0.74 at
decision 1) to minimum transport (0.80 at decision 5). The AGV head moves
from earliest free (0.41 at decision 1) to nearest (0.88 at decision 5). The
job head changes less: earliest finish stays near 0.4, and most remaining
operations falls from 0.10 to 0.04. The prior therefore shifts as the partial
schedule fills, which is the behaviour the rule selector is meant to produce.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Amortization against per-instance search}
RGA and MIP are better than the controller by 78.66 and 51.65 on the
$6\times6$ test set. The gap has a clear source. RGA evaluates 4850 schedules
per instance, and MIP solves two CP-SAT models with a 10~s and an 8~s
limit. The controller constructs one schedule per instance in a single pass
and takes about 0.56~s. Through the same simulator, one RGA run takes about
437.8~s; with a direct decoder, the same 4850 evaluations take 1.27~s. The
controller amortizes the search over the training
set; the baselines spend the search budget on each instance. The comparison
therefore measures two operating points, not two implementations of the same
search.

\paragraph{Anytime comparison}
Figure~\ref{fig:anytime} places the controller on the time--quality plane. At
0.56~s the controller obtains 556.70; an RGA run with the same budget, about
1600 evaluations, obtains 550.85. The crossover is about 0.5~s: below it the
controller is better, above it RGA is. The controller's advantage over PDR,
41.5\%, is the robust part of the comparison; its position against RGA depends
on the time budget.

\paragraph{Training dynamics}
RS and no RS both improve quickly in the first 50 episodes. After that, the
seed range remains wide: at the same episode, the three seeds can differ by
tens of points. The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:rs} is therefore made on the
selected checkpoint of each seed, not on the last episode.

\paragraph{Spread across instances}
The mean hides a difference in the tail. The per-instance standard deviation
of the seed-averaged controller values is 68.7; across individual seeds it
ranges from 73.7 to 87.5. RGA has 56.1 on the same basis, and MIP has
72.1. The controller's spread is wider than RGA's but not wider than MIP's.
The same pattern
appears in the validation readings: both configurations improve quickly in
the first 50 episodes and then oscillate. A single-pass policy has no mechanism to recover from a
bad early assignment on a hard instance.

\paragraph{Why the rule selector has a small and uneven effect}
The selector obtains 556.70 against 564.05 without it. It is better in two
of three seeds, and the mean difference is 7.35, about 1.3\%. The per-seed
differences are uneven: $+12.73$, $-2.86$, and $-31.93$ (RS minus no RS). The
selector makes
four to thirteen decisions per episode, so its update sees a few dozen
transitions per group. The variance across seeds is therefore large relative
to the mean effect. We report the comparison as an analysis and do not use it
as a headline claim.

\paragraph{Why the encoder matters more than the algorithm}
The encoder ablation spans 117.68 between the all-Set-Transformer and the
all-GNN configurations, and the ordering holds in all three seeds. The
algorithm ablation spans 78.10 on $6\times6$ and 29.87 on $10\times6$. The
encoder changes the representation that all three heads share; the algorithm
changes how the same representation is updated. At this budget, the
representation is the larger lever. The machine-internal attention accounts
for 65.32 of the encoder gap, which is consistent with the three feature
groups carrying complementary information: processing time, processing
energy, and transport distance.

\paragraph{Why GRPO differs from PPO here}
PPO trains a value critic for each head and a centralized critic for the team.
The update sees one group of eight episodes, so each critic is fitted to eight
returns per group while the policy heads are shared across all steps. The
critic's error therefore enters the advantage of every head. GRPO replaces the
critic with the within-group mean and standard deviation of the same eight
returns. The comparison at two sizes is consistent with this explanation: the
gap is larger at $6\times6$ (78.10) than at $10\times6$ (29.87), where each
episode contains more decisions and the critic has more data. The two
protocols also differ in weight sampling, one vector per group for GRPO and one
per environment for PPO, so the gap is not attributed to the critic alone.

\paragraph{What the controller is for}
The controller is better than the rule-based and the adapted DRL baselines at
every size. Against the search methods the result depends on the size: at
$6\times6$ RGA is ahead by 16.5\% and MIP by 10.2\%, and at $10\times10$ the
controller is ahead of both. The claim is therefore an operating point, not a
dominance: a feasible schedule in about half a second, for a shop that
reschedules frequently or evaluates many what-if scenarios, and a schedule
that stays competitive when the instance grows and the search methods slow
down.

\paragraph{Scope} Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the transport
assumptions (A1)--(A5). The MIP baseline is a two-stage heuristic, not a
joint optimum. All learned results use three training seeds.
