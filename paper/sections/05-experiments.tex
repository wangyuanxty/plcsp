\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

We use three instance families: $6\times6\times2$ (six jobs, six machines,
two workshops), $10\times6\times2$, and $10\times10\times2$. Each family has
100 test instances. All
methods are evaluated on the same instances, with the weights fixed to
$(0.4,0.4,0.2)$ in \eqref{eq:objective}. The learned policies are evaluated
greedily. Each learned method is trained with three seeds; the reported value
is the mean of the three seed-level test means. Rule-based and
metaheuristic baselines are deterministic given their own seed.

Every learned method gets 200 training episodes. The group
size
is 8, the instance batch is resampled every 8 episodes, and the learning rate
is $10^{-3}$ with Adam and no decay. These settings are identical across the
ablations; only the factor under test changes.

Instances are the published pools of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}, used unchanged: their training pool
(12{,}800 instances, seed 0), their validation pool (100, seed 1) and their
test pool (100, seed 3). The processing times, the processing powers, the
travel times and the workshop assignment are element-wise identical to the
released files, so every method in this paper is measured on the same
instances as the E2E baseline of that paper. We append two arrays to each
instance, the machine-to-station distances and the station-to-station
transfer times that the load/unload stations require; the four original
arrays are not modified. Each job has $M$ operations, and each operation is
eligible on a subset of machines. The mean processing time is drawn from
$U(1,99)$ and the mean processing power from $U(1,20)$; the value on a given
machine is the mean multiplied by a factor from $U(0.8,1.2)$. Same-workshop
travel times are drawn from $U(1,10)$ and cross-workshop travel times from
$U(10,20)$. The machines are split equally between the two workshops, and
each workshop has three AGVs. A checkpoint is selected on the validation set,
and the test set is used once after training.

The objective of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses the same four
terms. They set the standby power of the machines and the transport power of
the vehicles to one, so their energy terms equal the idle time and the loaded
transport time used here. The transport model differs: they assume that the
transport machines of a workshop are adequate, whereas here each workshop has
three AGVs and a cross-workshop trip includes a station transfer. So the absolute
objective values in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} are not directly
comparable with those reported there.

\subsection{Baselines}

The baselines are adaptations of published methods, not reproductions. Two of the papers release no code; those baselines are written from the paper and are labelled as adapted below. Every baseline is evaluated on the same instances and the same objective as the controller. The ablation study of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} uses the transport protocol of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} -- no AGV fleet, machine-to-machine travel, loaded travel only -- which is the protocol of their released code; Section~\ref{sec:e2eproto} explains why those values are not placed beside the published ones.

\paragraph{Rule-based} Twelve dispatching rules combine six job rules (FIFO,
MOR, and the most- and least-work-remaining forms on processing time and on
processing energy) with two machine rules (shortest processing time and
minimum processing energy), as in Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}. AGVs follow one of the two AGV rules the
controller selects from; the better one is reported.

\paragraph{RGA} A random-key genetic algorithm with population 50 and 100
generations, i.e.\ 4850 schedule evaluations per instance, with the operators
of Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2011rga}. It optimises the operation sequence and
the machine assignment; AGVs are dispatched by the earliest-arrival rule.
Every candidate schedule is scored by the same simulator that scores the
controller, so the search optimises exactly the objective of
\eqref{eq:objective} and its cost includes the evaluation cost. One run takes
3.07~s per instance at $6\times6$ and 2.97~s at $10\times6$.

\paragraph{MIP (joint CP-SAT)} The baseline is a mixed-integer program solved
with CP-SAT. One model decides the operation start times, the transport legs
and the AGV routes together, so routing is no longer decided after the
schedule is fixed, which is what separated the two-stage baseline from the
objective. The machine side is the no-overlap model of the FJSP; the transport
side is one route per AGV, encoded as a circuit over a depot and the legs the
AGV may serve, with the deadhead distance carried on each arc. A leg is an
entry leg from the load/unload station of the first operation's workshop, a
single leg between two machines of one workshop, or one of two legs of a
cross-workshop move, the two separated by the fixed delay of
\eqref{eq:split} that consumes no AGV. The objective is \eqref{eq:objective},
and the four components were checked against the simulator instance by
instance: the makespan, the energy and the trailing idle agree exactly.

The machine assignment is the one exception. It is taken from the no-AGV model
and held fixed; the fully joint variant, which leaves the assignment free, was
tried and does not close at these sizes within the time budget. The gap we
report is the gap of the transport-and-timing model given that
assignment, not the gap of the fully joint problem.

Each run uses a 60~s assignment stage and a 300~s cap on the joint model. At
$6\times6$ the model closes on all 100 test instances with a zero gap in 36~s
on average; at $10\times6$ and $10\times10$ it closes 92 and 91, returns a
feasible schedule for 6 and 8, and returns nothing for 2 and 1. The assignment
stage is time-limited, so this is one draw of the solver.

\paragraph{E2E} The architecture of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses a GIN job encoder and a GAT machine
encoder. We train it with PPO under the same protocol, with the nearest-vehicle
AGV rule. It is the published architecture retrained, not a reproduction of
the numbers in that paper.

\paragraph{Li et al.} The multi-agent method of Li et
al.~\cite{li2025plcsp} is adapted. Four actors (job filter, job selection,
machine selection, and AGV selection) each have their own critic and output
small rule-weight or proto-action vectors; a hand-written priority vector
makes the choice. The original problem has fixed machines and total weighted
tardiness; here the actors choose from the eligible machines and the
objective is \eqref{eq:objective}, and the due-date rules are replaced by
energy and transport rules. It is trained for 1500 episodes, the budget of
the original paper. The authors released no code; the baseline is written
from the paper and is not a reproduction.

\paragraph{Excluded baseline} The nested method of
Shi et al.~\cite{shi2025nested} was implemented and trained for 1000
episodes, but it is not reported in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}. The best
fixed rule in its action space attains 1008.93, 1184.86, and 2195.48 at the
three sizes. Three seeds of the learned policy give 1957.67, 2870.31, and
6292.75, and the ratio of the worst seed to the best spans 1.3 to 2.9. The
decoder agrees with the shared decoder to zero, so the spread comes from the
training procedure, not from the environment. One seed reaches 1255.72 at
$6\times6$; the other two give 2045.72 and 2571.56. We exclude the method
because this spread does not support a fair comparison.

\paragraph{Learned baselines} PPO with per-agent critics replaces GRPO in the
same architecture. The no-RS variant removes the top-level rule selector.

\subsection{Quality and run time}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the objective on the three instance
families. Every method is evaluated on the same instances and scored with the
same objective under the same transport model; the learned methods are averaged
over three seeds on the 100 test instances, and the rule-based rows are
deterministic.

The objective is not normalised for instance size. Its idle term is
$\sum_k(\mathsf{MKT}-P_k)$, which grows with the number of machines and the
makespan rather than with the work content, so a larger family can score lower
than a smaller one. The same non-monotonicity appears in the reference paper:
Wang et al. report 732.5 at $6\times6\times2$ and 674.5 at $10\times6\times2$
under the same objective structure. So we compare absolute values
within a column only, never across columns.

The MIP column is the mixed-integer program of
Section~\ref{sec:experiments}, solved with CP-SAT. It closes on all 100
instances with a proven zero gap at $6\times6$ and at $10\times6$; the
$10\times10$ run is still in progress. A two-stage variant, which fixes the
machine schedule first and routes the AGVs afterwards, was also built; it
obtains 881.43 at $6\times6$, that is, 13.68 worse than the controller and
263.39 worse than the joint model, so we report the joint model only.

Table~\ref{tab:pdr} lists the twelve rule pairs under the three AGV rules.
The best pair is mor/spt at $6\times6$ and $10\times10$ and mor/seyc at
$10\times6$, and the earliest-arrival rule is the best of the three at every
pair and size.

Three observations follow. First, the search methods are stronger where the
shop is small. At $6\times6$ the joint MIP obtains 605.77, which is 261.98
below the controller, and RGA obtains 762.04, which is 105.71 below; at
$10\times6$ the joint MIP is 292.24 below and RGA 11.97. Both pay for that
margin. RGA evaluates 4850 schedules per instance, and the joint MIP takes
36 and 204~s; one controller pass takes 0.95 and 1.99~s. Second, the ordering
reverses for RGA and the learned baselines but not for the solver. At
$10\times10$ the controller obtains 1836.64, which is 20.1\% below RGA and
below every learned baseline, while the joint MIP obtains 1242.46, which is
32.4\% below the controller. The reversal against RGA is consistent with its
configuration: one population size and one generation count at every size, so
its advantage over a fixed policy shrinks as the instance grows. That is a
limit on its generalization, not on its search. Third, the learned baselines are behind
at all three sizes. The retrained E2E architecture is 207.89, 257.57 and
1317.17 above the controller, and Li et al.\ is 292.84, 389.42 and 902.27
above it.

\paragraph{Significance} Table~\ref{tab:signif} reports a two-sided Wilcoxon
signed-rank test on the paired per-instance objectives, with Holm's correction
over the 15 comparisons; the learned methods enter as their seed mean on each
instance. The controller is lower than
PDR, E2E, and Li et al.\ at every size, at $p<10^{-16}$, on 97 to 100 of the
100 instances, and the joint MIP is lower at every size, on 100, 98 and 99 of
them. The disagreement is confined to RGA. At $6\times6$ RGA is lower, on
84 instances; at $10\times10$ the controller is lower, on 97. At $10\times6$
the test does not separate the two (median 6.92, controller lower
on 43), so the mean gap of 11.97 is not resolved by the paired comparison.

\subsection{Absolute values against the published ones}
\label{sec:e2eproto}

Wang et al.\ use two idle readings in their two tables: their rule-based table
charges only the gap before a machine's first operation and between its
operations, and their mixed-integer table also charges the interval from a
machine's last operation to the makespan. Re-running their code reproduces the
rule table to within 0.004\% under the first reading and misses it by 19--21\%
under the second. This paper uses the second reading, because a machine is
idle until the job leaves the shop.

Measured on their instances and their transport protocol under the first
reading, our controller obtains $559.31 \pm 12.24$ at $6\times6$ and
$575.27 \pm 4.12$ at $10\times6$, and the all-GNN encoder of their
architecture, retrained here under the same protocol, obtains
$676.99 \pm 14.49$ and $678.88 \pm 91.75$. The values published for P-G are
732.5 and 674.5.

These values are not carried into Table~\ref{tab:baselines} or
Table~\ref{tab:encoder}, which use the second reading throughout; the encoder
ordering reported there is an internal comparison and does not depend on the
reading.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}

Both ablations in this subsection are run under the protocol of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}: no AGV fleet,
machine-to-machine travel, and loaded travel only. That is the protocol of the
E2E baseline in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}, so the encoder and the estimator
are compared with their architecture on the same footing rather than in a
regime where only one side was measured. The AGV agent and the rule selector
are not part of these ablations; each is removed from the full model
separately below.

\paragraph{Encoder} Table~\ref{tab:encoder} reports a $2\times2$ factorial
over the job and machine encoders at $6\times6$, without AGVs and without the
rule selector. The all-ST cell obtains 798.06 and the job-GNN/machine-ST cell
797.11; the two are indistinguishable, because the all-ST cell is better in
one seed out of three and the 0.95 difference is far inside the seed spread.
Machine encoding behaves differently. Replacing the ST machine encoder by a
GNN costs
85.80 with the job encoder at ST and 128.10 with the job encoder at GNN, and
the ordering holds in all three seeds at both job settings. For the job
encoder we find no such main effect: its cost appears only at the GNN machine
setting. The interaction is not small, so we do not report a
job-encoder main effect. Machine-internal attention is a design detail inside the ST
machine encoder: removing it costs 47.75 (845.81 against 798.06). The
$10\times6$ encoder cells were deferred and are not reported.

\paragraph{Rule usage}
The mean probability of each rule at the five top-level decision points, over
20 test instances (seed 1), shows that the selector is not a fixed rule. The
machine rule head moves from minimum processing time (0.55 at decision 1) to

at decision 5). No arm keeps one rule above 0.85, so the prior
shifts as the partial schedule fills, which is the behaviour the rule
selector is meant to produce.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Amortization against per-instance search}
RGA evaluates 4850 schedules per instance, each scored by the simulator, and
the joint MIP takes 36~s per instance. The controller constructs one schedule
in a single pass and takes about 0.95~s. RGA buys its margin with
roughly three times the compute of one forward pass, and the MIP with
thirty-eight times. The controller amortizes the search over the training set;
the baselines spend the search budget on each instance. The
comparison measures two cost budgets, not two implementations
of the same search, and it does not support a claim that our controller is
faster than RGA at this size.

\paragraph{Training dynamics}
RS and no RS both improve quickly in the first 50 episodes. After that, the
seed range remains wide: at the same episode, the three seeds can differ by
tens of points. So we compare the selected checkpoint of each seed in
Table~\ref{tab:rs}, not the last episode.

\paragraph{Spread across instances}
A difference in the tail is hidden by the mean. The per-instance standard deviation
of the seed-averaged controller values is 109.9, and across individual seeds it
ranges from 127.0 to 140.0. RGA has 79.7 on the same basis and MIP has
128.5, so the controller's spread is wider than RGA's and close to MIP's.
The same pattern appears in the validation readings, and a single-pass policy
has no mechanism to recover from a
bad early assignment on a hard instance.

\paragraph{Why the rule selector has a small effect}
The mean difference is 27.46, or 3.1\%, at $6\times6$, and the selector is
better in all three seeds there.
Per-seed
differences are $-29.82$, $-15.61$, and $-36.97$ (RS minus no RS), so
the sign is stable while the size varies by a factor of 2.4. At $10\times6$
the mean difference falls to 10.66 and the sign is no longer stable. The selector makes a
few decisions per episode, so its update sees a few dozen transitions per
group. The effect is real but small next to the 451.49 that separates the
controller from the best PDR pair. We report the comparison as an analysis
and do not use it as a headline claim.

\paragraph{Why the encoder matters more than the algorithm}
Under the protocol of Wang et al.\ the encoder ablation spans 127.15 between
the all-Set-Transformer and the all-GNN configurations, and the ordering holds
in all three seeds; under the full protocol the same span is 96.47. The
algorithm ablation spans 72.98 at $6\times6$ under the first protocol and
98.38 under the second. Whereas the
encoder changes the representation that all three agents share, the algorithm
changes how the same representation is updated. Representation is the
larger lever in the first protocol and the smaller one in the second, so we do
not claim a general ordering between the two. Machine-internal attention
accounts for 47.75 of the encoder gap, which is consistent with the three
feature groups carrying complementary information: processing time, processing
energy, and transport distance.

\paragraph{Why GRPO differs from PPO here}
PPO trains a value critic for each agent and a centralized critic for the team.
The update sees one group of eight episodes, so each critic is fitted to eight
returns per group while the actor networks are shared across all steps. So the
critic's error enters the advantage of every agent. GRPO replaces
those critics by the within-group mean and standard deviation of the same
eight returns, so no critic is fitted at the low level. The gap is 72.98 at
$6\times6$ under the protocol of Wang et al., 98.38 at $6\times6$ under the
full protocol and 56.02 at $10\times6$. It does not grow with the instance, so
we do not claim a trend with size. A paired test on the 100 instances gives
$p=9.0\times10^{-16}$ at $6\times6$ and $p=1.4\times10^{-9}$ at $10\times6$,
with GRPO lower on 90 and 77 of them. Both algorithms used one weight vector
per group, so the gap is attributed to the estimator.

\paragraph{What the controller is for}
We do not claim the best objective at every size. The controller is better
than the rule-based and the adapted DRL baselines at every size, but two
search methods are ahead of it. RGA obtains 762.04 on the $6\times6$ test set
against 867.75 for the controller, so it is 12.2\% ahead, and the joint MIP is
ahead of the controller at all three sizes. The search methods pay
for that quality
in online time. Under the same budget of 4850 schedule evaluations per
instance, and with every candidate scored by the same simulator the controller
is scored with, one RGA run takes 3.07~s per instance at $6\times6$ against
0.95~s for one pass of the controller. The cost of the search grows steeply
with the instance: on the instances of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} their RGA needs 6.39~s per instance at
$6\times6$, 19.94~s at $10\times6$ and 186.3~s at $10\times10$, while one
controller pass takes 0.95, 1.99 and 4.16~s. The
mixed-integer route fails differently. Our joint model is solved with the
machine assignment fixed first; the variant that leaves the assignment free was
tried and does not close at these sizes, and even with the assignment fixed a
$10\times10$ instance takes 210~s on average. The claim is
an operating point rather than a dominance: a feasible schedule in about a
second, for a shop that reschedules often or evaluates many what-if scenarios,
and a method whose cost grows slowly with the instance size.

\paragraph{Scope} Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the transport
assumptions (A1)--(A5). The joint MIP is solved to proven optimality on all
100 instances at $6\times6$ and on 92 and 91 of the larger sizes; the others
either return a feasible schedule or nothing within the cap. Its machine
assignment is fixed in all three sizes.
All learned results use three training seeds.
