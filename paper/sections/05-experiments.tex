\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

We use three instance families: $6\times6\times2$ (six jobs, six machines,
two workshops), $10\times6\times2$, and $10\times10\times2$. Each family has
100 test instances. All
methods are evaluated on the same instances, with the weights fixed to
$(0.4,0.4,0.2)$ in \eqref{eq:objective}. The learned policies are evaluated
greedily. Each learned method is trained with three seeds; the reported value
is the mean of the three seed-level test means. The rule-based and
metaheuristic baselines are deterministic given their own seed.

The training budget is 200 episodes for every learned method. The group size
is 8, the instance batch is resampled every 8 episodes, and the learning rate
is $10^{-3}$ with Adam and no decay. These settings are identical across the
ablations; only the factor under test changes.

\subsection{Baselines}

\paragraph{Rule-based} Twelve dispatching rules combine four job rules and
three machine rules. AGVs follow the earliest-free rule. The best rule pair
is reported.

\paragraph{RGA} A random-key genetic algorithm with population 50 and 100
generations, i.e.\ 4850 schedule evaluations per instance. It uses the same
decision space as the learned controller: machine assignment and AGV
dispatch, without an explicit operation sequence.

\paragraph{MIP} A two-stage mixed-integer baseline. Stage~A solves the
machine assignment and the operation start times with a CP-SAT model. Stage~B
routes the AGVs with a second CP-SAT model that assigns each transport leg to
a workshop fleet and sequences the legs of each AGV. The two stages are
alternated three times, and the best schedule is kept. The cost model of both
stages is identical to \eqref{eq:objective}, including the idle term
\eqref{eq:idle} and the handover split \eqref{eq:split}. The time limit is
10~s for Stage~A and 8~s for each Stage~B solve.

\paragraph{B3} The multi-action DRL framework of Lei et
al.~\cite{lei2022} is evaluated with its pretrained $6\times6$ weights: the
job and machine actors are used greedily, the AGV is dispatched by the
earliest-free rule, and the machine input is zero-padded to our feature
width. We report it as an adapted baseline, not as a reproduction.

\paragraph{E2E} The architecture of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses a GIN job encoder and a GAT machine
encoder. We train it with PPO under the same protocol, with the earliest-free
AGV rule. It is the published architecture retrained, not a reproduction of
the numbers in that paper.

\paragraph{Learned baselines} PPO with per-head critics replaces GRPO in the
same architecture. The no-RS variant removes the top-level rule selector.

\subsection{Scale test at $10\times6$}

At $10\times6$ the full model obtains 576.92, against 575.27 for the same
controller without the selector and the AGV head. The difference is 1.65,
smaller than the seed range of the full model (11.33), so neither component
changes the objective at this size. The controller beats PDR by 524.38 and B3
by 74.38, and is 11.29 behind RGA.

\paragraph{Rule usage}
The mean probability of each rule at the five top-level decision points, over
20 test instances (seed 1), shows that the selector is not a fixed rule. The
machine head moves from minimum energy (0.74 at
decision 1) to minimum transport (0.80 at decision 5). The AGV head moves
from earliest free (0.41 at decision 1) to nearest (0.88 at decision 5). The
job head changes less: earliest finish stays near 0.4, and most remaining
operations falls from 0.10 to 0.04. The prior therefore shifts as the partial
schedule fills, which is the behaviour the rule selector is meant to produce.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Amortization against per-instance search}
RGA and MIP are better than the controller by 78.66 and 51.65 on the
$6\times6$ test set. The gap has a clear source. RGA evaluates 4850 schedules
per instance, and MIP solves two CP-SAT models with a 10~s and an 8~s
limit. The controller constructs one schedule per instance in a single pass
and takes about 0.56~s. The controller amortizes the search over the training
set; the baselines spend the search budget on each instance. The comparison
therefore measures two operating points, not two implementations of the same
search.

\paragraph{Training dynamics}
RS and no RS both improve quickly in the first 50 episodes. After that, the
seed range remains wide: at the same episode, the three seeds can differ by
tens of points. The comparison in Table~\ref{tab:rs} is therefore made on the
selected checkpoint of each seed, not on the last episode.

\paragraph{Spread across instances}
The mean hides a difference in the tail. The per-instance standard deviation
of the seed-averaged controller values is 68.7; across individual seeds it
ranges from 73.7 to 87.5. RGA has 56.1 on the same basis, and MIP has
72.1. The controller is not only worse on average; it also has a longer tail
on the hard instances. The same pattern
appears in the validation readings: both configurations improve quickly in
the first 50 episodes and then oscillate. A single-pass policy has no mechanism to recover from a
bad early assignment on a hard instance.

\paragraph{Why the rule selector has a small and uneven effect}
The selector obtains 556.70 against 564.05 without it. It is better in two
of three seeds, and the mean difference is 7.35, about 1.3\%. The per-seed
differences are uneven: $+12.73$, $-2.86$, and $-31.93$ (RS minus no RS). The
selector makes
four to seven decisions per episode, so its update sees a few dozen
transitions per group. The variance across seeds is therefore large relative
to the mean effect. We report the comparison as an analysis and do not use it
as a headline claim.

\paragraph{Why the encoder matters more than the algorithm}
The encoder ablation spans 117.68 between the all-Set-Transformer and the
all-GNN configurations, and the ordering holds in all three seeds. The
algorithm ablation spans 78.10 on $6\times6$ and 29.87 on $10\times6$. The
encoder changes the representation that all three heads share; the algorithm
changes how the same representation is updated. At this budget, the
representation is the larger lever. The machine-internal attention accounts
for 65.32 of the encoder gap, which is consistent with the three feature
groups carrying complementary information: processing time, processing
energy, and transport distance.

\paragraph{Why GRPO is more stable than PPO here}
PPO trains a critic for each head and a centralized critic for the team. The
top level makes only four to seven decisions per episode, and the low-level
group contains eight episodes. The critic therefore has few targets per
update, and its error enters the advantage of every head. GRPO replaces the
critic with the within-group mean and standard deviation, which are computed
from the same eight returns. The comparison at two sizes is consistent with
this explanation: the gap is larger at $6\times6$ (78.10) than at
$10\times6$ (29.87), where each episode contains more decisions and the
critic has more data.

\paragraph{What the controller is for}
The results do not support a claim that the controller dominates the
baselines. They support a narrower claim: the controller returns a feasible
schedule in about half a second, and its quality is within 11\% of a
two-stage mixed-integer baseline that takes about 22 times longer. For a shop
that reschedules frequently or evaluates many what-if scenarios, this
operating point is useful. For a single instance with a large time budget,
RGA or MIP is the better choice.

\paragraph{Scope} Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the transport
assumptions (A1)--(A5). The MIP baseline is a two-stage heuristic, not a
joint optimum. All learned results use three training seeds.
