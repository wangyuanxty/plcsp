\section{Experiments}

\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Protocol}

We consider five instance families of the form $J \times M \times W$, where $J$, $M$, and $W$ denote 
the numbers of jobs, machines, and workshops respectively: $6 \times 6 \times 2$, $10 \times 6 \times 2$, 
$10 \times 10 \times 2$, $15 \times 10 \times 2$, and $20 \times 6 \times 3$.
Each family has 100 test instances. All methods are evaluated on the same instances, with the weights fixed to
$(0.4,0.4,0.2)$ in \eqref{eq:objective}. The learned policies are evaluated
greedily. Each learned method is trained with three seeds and the reported value
is the mean of the three seed-level test means. Rule-based and
metaheuristic baselines are deterministic given their own seed.

Our model, the ablations and the retrained baseline of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} use 200 training
episodes; Li et al.\ uses the budget of its own paper, as noted below. The group
size is 8, the instance batch is resampled every 8 episodes, and the learning rate
is $10^{-3}$ with Adam and no decay. These settings are identical across the
ablations, only the factor under test changes.

We adopt the public instance pools of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} without modification: a training pool of 12{,}800 instances (seed 0), 
a validation pool of 100 instances (seed 1), and a test pool of 100 instances (seed 3). 
The processing times, the processing powers, the
travel times and the workshop assignment are element-wise identical to the
released files, so every method in this paper is measured on the same
instances as the baseline of that paper. We append two arrays to each
instance, the machine-to-station distances and the station-to-station
transfer times that the load/unload stations require; the four original
arrays are not modified. Each job has $M$ operations, and each operation is
eligible on a subset of machines. The mean processing time is drawn from
$U(1,99)$ and the mean processing power from $U(1,20)$; the value on a given
machine is the mean multiplied by a factor from $U(0.8,1.2)$. Same-workshop
travel times are drawn from $U(1,10)$ and cross-workshop travel times from
$U(10,20)$. The machines are split equally between the two workshops, and
each workshop has three AGVs. A checkpoint is selected on the validation set,
and the test set is used once after training.

The objective of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses the same four
terms. They set the standby power of the machines and the transport power of
the vehicles to one, so their energy terms equal the idle time and the loaded
transport time used here. The transport model differs: they assume that the
transport machines of a workshop are adequate, whereas here each workshop has
three AGVs and a cross-workshop trip includes a station transfer. So the absolute
objective values in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} are not directly
comparable with those reported there.

\subsection{Baselines}

The baselines are adaptations of published methods, not reproductions. Since two of the papers release no code,
the corresponding baselines were reproduced from the paper descriptions and are marked as adapted below.
Every baseline is evaluated on the same instances and the same objective as our model. The ablation study of
Section~\ref{sec:ablations} uses the transport protocol of Wang et al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} --
no AGV fleet, machine-to-machine travel, loaded travel only -- which is the protocol of their released code;
Section~\ref{sec:published-values} explains why those values are not placed beside the published ones.

\paragraph{Rule-based (PDR)} A priority dispatching rule combines six job rules (FIFO,
MOR, and the most- and least-work-remaining forms on processing time and on
processing energy) with two machine rules (shortest processing time and
minimum processing energy), as in Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget}. AGVs follow one of the three AGV rules our model selects from; the better one is reported.

\paragraph{RGA} A random-key genetic algorithm with population 50 and 100
generations, i.e.\ 4850 schedule evaluations per instance, with the operators
of Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang2011rga}. It optimises the operation sequence and
the machine assignment. AGVs are dispatched by the earliest-arrival rule.
Every candidate schedule is scored by the same simulator that scores our model, so the search optimises exactly the objective of
\eqref{eq:objective} and its cost includes the evaluation cost. The computational time per instance is 
437.8~s per instance at $6\times6$, 1426~s at $10\times6$ and 4161~s at
$10\times10$.

\paragraph{Wang et al.} The
architecture~\cite{wang2025multitarget} uses a GIN (graph isomorphism network)
job encoder and a GAT (graph attention network) machine encoder. We train it with PPO under the same protocol, with the nearest-vehicle
AGV rule. It is the published architecture retrained, not a reproduction of
the numbers in that paper.

\paragraph{Li et al.} The multi-agent method of Li et
al.~\cite{li2025plcsp} is adapted. Four actors (job filter, job selection,
machine selection, and AGV selection) each have their own critic and output
small rule-weight or proto-action vectors; a hand-written priority vector
makes the choice. The original problem has fixed machines and total weighted
tardiness; here the actors choose from the eligible machines and the
objective is \eqref{eq:objective}, and the due-date rules are replaced by
energy and transport rules. It is trained for 1500 episodes, the budget of
the original paper. The authors released no code; the baseline is written
from the paper and is not a reproduction.

\paragraph{Excluded baseline} The nested method of
Shi et al.~\cite{shi2025nested} was implemented and trained for 1000
episodes, but it is not reported in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}. The best
fixed rule in its action space attains 1008.93, 1184.86, and 2195.48 at the
three sizes. Three seeds of the learned policy give 1957.67, 2870.31, and
6292.75, and the ratio of the worst seed to the best spans 1.3 to 2.9. The
decoder agrees with the shared decoder to zero, so the spread comes from the
training procedure, not from the environment. One seed reaches 1255.72 at
$6\times6$; the other two give 2045.72 and 2571.56. We exclude the method
because this spread does not support a fair comparison.

\paragraph{Learned baselines} PPO with per-agent critics replaces GRPO in the
same architecture. The variant without the rule selector removes the top-level
component.

\subsection{Quality and run time}

Table~\ref{tab:baselines} reports the objective on the five instance
families. Every method is evaluated on the same instances and scored with the
same objective under the same transport model. The learned methods are averaged
over three seeds on the 100 test instances, and the rule-based rows are
deterministic.

The objective is not normalised for instance size. By
\eqref{eq:idlelinear} the idle term is $M\Cmax-\sum t$, so the coefficient of
the makespan is $w_1+w_2M$: 2.8 at $M=6$ and 4.4 at $M=10$. The idle term grows
with the number of machines and the makespan rather than with the work
content, so a larger family can score lower than a smaller one, and the
trade-off itself moves with the number of machines. The same non-monotonicity
appears in the reference paper: Wang et al. report 732.5 at $6\times6\times2$
and 674.5 at $10\times6\times2$ under the same objective structure. So we
compare absolute values within a column only, never across columns.

In addition, three observations follow. First, RGA is stronger where the shop is small. At
$6\times6$ it obtains 762.04, which is 105.71 below our model, and at
$10\times6$ it is 11.97 below. It pays for that margin: one run evaluates 4850
schedules per instance, against 0.95 and 1.99~s for one pass of our model.
Second, the ordering reverses as the instance grows. At
$10\times10$ our model obtains 1836.64, which is 20.1\% below RGA, at
$15\times10$ it obtains 1542.33, which is 42.7\% below RGA, and at
$20\times6$ it obtains 994.55, which is 15.8\% below RGA; it is also
below every learned baseline at these sizes. The reversal is consistent with the
configuration of RGA: one population size and one generation count at every
size, so its advantage over a fixed policy shrinks as the instance grows. That
is a limit on its generalization, not on its search. Third, the learned baselines are behind
at all five sizes. The retrained architecture of Wang et al.\ is 207.89, 257.57,
1317.17, 1283.68 and 235.15 above our model, and Li et al.\ is 292.84, 389.42, 902.27,
916.36 and 315.26 above it. 

Table~\ref{tab:pdr} lists the twelve job--machine rule pairs, each run under
the three AGV rules, so it has 36 rows per family. The best pair is mor/spt at
$6\times6\times2$, $10\times10\times2$ and $20\times6\times3$, and mor/seyc at
$10\times6\times2$ and $15\times10\times2$. The earliest-arrival AGV rule is
the best of the three for every pair and every family. Table~\ref{tab:time} reports the wall-clock time to
build one schedule for every method and family. Every row is timed through the same simulator, one process at a time. 
The learned methods run on the GPU, the others on one CPU core. 
PDR applies a single pair of rules, whereas RGA performs a full run of 4850 schedule evaluations. 
Therefore, the cost reported for RGA corresponds to the entire run. Figure~\ref{fig:radar}
summarises the same means on six axes; each axis is min-max normalised inside
its panel, and the outward direction is the better value.

\paragraph{Significance} Table~\ref{tab:baselines} compares methods by the
mean objective over the 100 test instances, and a gap between two means may
simply reflect which instances were drawn. Table~\ref{tab:signif} therefore
tests every pair of methods instance by instance. For each instance we subtract
the baseline's objective from ours, and apply a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank
test to the resulting 100 differences, which asks whether they are
systematically different from zero. The learned methods enter as the mean of
their three training seeds on each instance, so that each method contributes
one value per instance. The 20 tests, five sizes against four baselines, are
corrected together with Holm's procedure at $\alpha=0.05$.

The result is the same against three of the four baselines: our model has a
lower objective than PDR and than the adapted baselines of Wang et al.\ and
Li et al.\ at every size, with $p<10^{-16}$ after correction, and it is lower
on 97 to 100 of the 100 instances. RGA is the only baseline whose
result changes with size. At $6\times6$ RGA is lower on 84 of the 100
instances, with $p=2.2\times10^{-13}$, so RGA is the better of the two there.
At $10\times10$, $15\times10$ and $20\times6$ our model is lower on 97, 100
and 97 instances, all with $p<10^{-16}$. At $10\times6$ the test finds no
significant difference: our model is lower on 43 of the 100 instances, the
median difference is 6.92 in RGA's favour, and $p=0.312$, so RGA's mean
advantage of 11.97 in Table~\ref{tab:baselines} is not significant at this
size.

Figure~\ref{fig:distribution} compares the objective in two scenarios: with and without a selector.
The top row pools the 300 values of each configuration (100 instances, three
seeds). At $6\times6\times2$ the mean falls from 895.21 to 867.75, at
$10\times6\times2$ from 819.33 to 808.67, and at $10\times10\times2$ from
1880.40 to 1836.64. In all three panels the interquartile bar, the median and
the mean move down together, while the violins retain their shape and overlap
over almost the entire range.

The bottom row splits the objective into its terms. Each axis is divided by
its value without the selector, so the dashed pentagon is the baseline and the
outside is better. At $6\times6\times2$ the selector lowers every term: Cmax by
2.4\%, processing energy by 1.8\%, transport time by 6.1\%, idle energy by
3.7\% and the objective by 3.1\%. At $10\times6\times2$ Cmax, idle energy and
the objective fall by 1.0\%, 4.3\% and 1.3\%, but transport time rises by
4.6\% and processing energy by 0.2\%. At $10\times10\times2$ Cmax, idle energy
and the objective fall by 1.4\%, 3.2\% and 2.3\%, while transport time rises by
0.6\% and processing energy by 0.2\%, so the gain is a trade-off rather than a
uniform improvement at the two larger families. The seed means behind the
objective separate the two: at $6\times6\times2$ the three seeds gain 29.8,
15.6 and 37.0 points, and even the worst selector seed (880.92) is lower than
the best seed without it (887.75); at $10\times6\times2$ the differences are
31.7, $+13.4$ and 13.6, where seed 2 reverses; at $10\times10\times2$ they are
44.0, 82.8 and 4.4, all in favour of the selector. The selector is a small,
distribution-wide gain that is reliable at $6\times6\times2$ and at
$10\times10\times2$.

\subsection{Absolute values against the published ones}
\label{sec:published-values}

Wang et al.\ use two idle readings in their two tables: their rule-based table
charges only the gap before a machine's first operation and between its
operations, and their mixed-integer table also charges the interval from a
machine's last operation to the makespan. Re-running their code reproduces the
rule table to within 0.004\% under the first reading and misses it by 19--21\%
under the second. This paper uses the second reading, because a machine is
idle until the job leaves the shop.

Measured on their instances and their transport protocol under the first
reading, our model obtains $559.31 \pm 12.24$ at $6\times6$ and
$575.27 \pm 4.12$ at $10\times6$, and the all-GNN encoder of their
architecture, retrained here under the same protocol, obtains
$676.99 \pm 14.49$ and $678.88 \pm 91.75$. The values published for P-G are
732.5 and 674.5.

These values are not carried into Table~\ref{tab:baselines} or
Table~\ref{tab:encoder}, which use the second reading throughout; the encoder
ordering reported there is an internal comparison and does not depend on the
reading.

\subsection{Analysis}

\paragraph{Amortization against per-instance search}
RGA evaluates 4850 schedules per instance, each scored by the same simulator
that scores our model. Our model constructs one schedule in a single
pass and takes about 0.95~s. One RGA run takes 437.8~s at $6\times6$, 1426~s
at $10\times6$ and 4161~s at $10\times10$, which is 461, 717 and 1000 times
one pass. Our model amortizes the search over the training set; RGA
spends the search budget on each instance.

\paragraph{Equal-budget comparison}
We cut the RGA budget and record the objective at each point. At $6\times6$ our model obtains 867.75 in one pass. RGA reaches the same objective at about
1{,}400 evaluations: it obtains 932.83 at 914, 868.20 at 1{,}394 and 803.85 at
2{,}306. One environment evaluation costs 0.091~s, so 1{,}394 evaluations need
about 127~s, which is 134 times one pass of 0.95~s. At its full budget of
4{,}850 evaluations, 437.8~s, RGA is 12.2\% better. The search buys its margin
by spending two orders of magnitude more online time.
Figure~\ref{fig:budget} shows the curve.

at five budgets; the dashed line is one pass of our model, which has no budget

\paragraph{Training dynamics}
The validation objective falls from 967 at episode 25 to 923 at episode 50 and
then stays between 890 and 930 (Figure~\ref{fig:curve}). The seeds converge
over the same run: they differ by 186 points at episode 25 and by 13 at episode
200. The best checkpoint still falls at a different episode for each seed, 125,
150 and 200, so we select one checkpoint per seed on the validation set rather
than take the last episode.

\paragraph{Spread across instances}
A difference in the tail is hidden by the mean. The per-instance standard deviation
of the seed-averaged values of our model is 109.9, and across individual seeds it
ranges from 127.0 to 140.0. RGA has 79.7 on the same basis, so the
seed-averaged spread of our model is 38\% wider.
The same pattern appears in the validation readings, and a single-pass policy
has no mechanism to recover from a
bad early assignment on a hard instance.

\paragraph{Why the rule selector has a small effect}
The mean difference is 27.46, or 3.1\%, at $6\times6$, and the selector is
better in all three seeds there.
Per-seed
differences are $-29.82$, $-15.61$, and $-36.97$ (with selector minus
without), so
the sign is stable while the size varies by a factor of 2.4. At $10\times10$
the mean difference is 43.76, or 2.3\%, and all three seeds favour the
selector, with per-seed differences of $-44.00$, $-82.84$ and $-4.44$; the
sign is again stable, but the size varies by a factor of 19. At $10\times6$
the mean difference falls to 10.66 and the sign is no longer stable. The selector makes a
few decisions per episode, so its update sees a few dozen transitions per
group. The effect is real but small next to the 451.49 that separates our model from the best PDR pair. We report the comparison as an analysis
and do not use it as a headline claim.

\paragraph{Why the encoder matters more than the algorithm}
Under the protocol of Wang et al.\ the encoder ablation spans 127.15 between
the all-Set-Transformer and the all-GNN configurations, and the ordering holds
in all three seeds; under the full protocol the same span is 96.47 at
$6\times6$ and 99.78 at $10\times6$. The
algorithm ablation spans 72.98 at $6\times6$ under the first protocol and
98.38 under the second. Whereas the
encoder changes the representation that all three agents share, the algorithm
changes how the same representation is updated. Representation is the
larger lever in the first protocol and the smaller one in the second, so we do
not claim a general ordering between the two. Machine-internal attention
accounts for 47.75 of the encoder gap, which is consistent with the three
feature groups carrying complementary information: processing time, processing
energy, and transport distance.

\paragraph{Why GRPO differs from PPO here}
PPO trains a value critic for each agent and a centralized critic for the team.
The update sees one group of eight episodes, so each critic is fitted to eight
returns per group while the actor networks are shared across all steps. So the
critic's error enters the advantage of every agent. GRPO replaces
those critics by the within-group mean and standard deviation of the same
eight returns, so no critic is fitted at the low level. The gap is 72.98 at
$6\times6$ under the protocol of Wang et al., 98.38 at $6\times6$ under the
full protocol, 56.02 at $10\times6$ and 280.14 at $10\times10$. The gap is
therefore not monotone in size: it falls from $6\times6$ to $10\times6$ and
rises at $10\times10$, so we do not claim a trend with size. A paired test on
the 100 instances, with Holm correction over the three sizes, gives
$p=9.0\times10^{-16}$ at $6\times6$, $p=1.4\times10^{-9}$ at $10\times6$ and
$p=1.8\times10^{-16}$ at $10\times10$, with GRPO lower on 90, 77 and 96 of
them. Both algorithms used one weight vector
per group, so the gap is attributed to the estimator.

\paragraph{What our model is for}
We do not claim the best objective at every size. Our model is better
than the rule-based and the adapted DRL baselines at every size, but RGA is
ahead of it at $6\times6$. RGA obtains 762.04 on that test set against 867.75
for our model, so it is 12.2\% ahead. The search pays for that quality in
online time. Under the same budget of 4850 schedule evaluations per instance,
and with every candidate scored by the same simulator our model is scored
with, one RGA run takes 437.8~s per instance at $6\times6$ against 0.95~s for
one pass of our model. The cost of the search grows steeply with the
instance. In the implementation of Wang et
al.~\cite{wang2025multitarget} their RGA needs 638.8, 1994.0 and 5590.3~s per
instance at the three sizes, against 0.529, 0.883 and 2.443~s for their learned
policy. The two implementations agree on the order of magnitude at every size.
The claim is an operating point
rather than a dominance: a feasible schedule in about a second, for a shop
that reschedules often or evaluates many what-if scenarios, and a method whose
cost grows slowly with the instance size.

\paragraph{Scope} Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the transport
assumptions (A1)--(A5).
Assumptions (A3)--(A5) leave charging, vehicle failure, and load/unload time
out of the model, so no number in this section includes them; a shop where
they matter would need an energy term per AGV and a state variable for its
charge.
The table values use three training seeds. The rule-usage analysis and the
case study read one seed, and say so where they are reported.




\subsection{One repair in the middle of the schedule}
\label{sec:repair}

The protocol above has no failures, by assumption~(A3). We relax that
assumption once, as a separate scenario, to see what each method does when a
resource needs a repair halfway through.

At step 14 of 36, the machine that step used needs 100 time units of repair.
Every operation the method places on that machine later pays the repair; the
vehicle that made the last trip pays it too. Machine and vehicle stay in the
candidate set, so a method that re-decides at every step can take another one,
and a method that fixes its plan up front cannot. Nothing is retrained for this
scenario: the same checkpoints, and each baseline is re-run inside the
experiment. The repair falls on the
machine each method used itself at step 14, so the three methods meet the same
kind of disruption rather than the same machine.

Table~\ref{tab:weights} gives the result. At $6\times6$, eight of the nine
points fall between 9.9 and 19.0\% above RGA, and the default weights of
Table~\ref{tab:baselines} sit at 12.2\%, so the relative position of our model
does not drift with the weights. The exception is transport alone, where our
model is 88.7\% above RGA; without the makespan and the energy terms our model
travels about twice as far. At $10\times6$ our model is above RGA at all nine
points, by 1.9 to 128.3\%: 1.9\% on makespan alone, 3.2\% on energy alone,
128.3\% on transport alone, and 2.7 to 17.5\% on the mixed points. At
$10\times10$ the sign reverses: our model is
below RGA at all nine points, by 11.6 to 30.6\%. The smallest margin is on
makespan alone (11.6\%) and the largest on transport alone (30.6\%). What the
nine points buy is not a smaller gap but a gap that does not need a new run:
RGA is re-solved at each point, 437.8~s per instance at $6\times6$, 1426~s at
$10\times6$ and 4161~s at $10\times10$, against 0.95, 1.99 and 4.16~s for one
pass of our model.
