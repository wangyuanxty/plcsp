\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Flexible job shop scheduling is a core problem in modern manufacturing,
especially in automotive parts production, 3C electronics assembly, and
semiconductor wafer fabrication. These plants typically consist of multiple
workshops, and automated guided vehicles (AGVs) are widely used to move jobs
between them, so transport becomes part of scheduling. In these settings, 
machine availability, energy consumption, and makespan all depend on how
processing and transportation resources are coordinated. However, existing
work rarely considers these aspects together. Making the three decisions
jointly is difficult. Rule-based methods commit to one dispatching policy and
cannot adapt to the shop state. Many learning-based methods separate the
decisions into stages. At each step the candidate sets change size, and the
three decisions share one return, which a per-agent critic cannot attribute. The performance of
a multi-workshop system depends not only on how machines are scheduled 
but also on how AGVs are dispatched and coordinated across different workshops. Besides, little attention has
been paid to multi-workshop environments with AGVs, where
jobs must be transferred across different workshops.
This creates a non-trivial decision problem: whether to send a job from the
current workshop immediately, or to wait for a job sent from the other
workshop. Existing research on integrated production and transportation
scheduling has mainly focused on single-workshop settings, where the
interaction between production resources and AGVs is relatively
straightforward. Less attention has been paid to multi-workshop settings, where
jobs must be transferred across workshop boundaries.

Consider a factory with two workshops and six machines, where a job is processed
on a machine in the first workshop and then on a machine in the second. The
second machine is free, but the job is not, because it waits for a vehicle.
The first workshop has an idle vehicle, and the second workshop has a busy one.
If the dispatcher sends the idle vehicle, the job arrives
at the boundary and waits for the second fleet; or it waits for a
vehicle of the second workshop instead, the first machine stays
idle. The dispatching decision affects the makespan, the idle energy consumption of both workshops, 
and the loaded travel time. A nearest-vehicle-first rule cannot capture this trade-off, 
whereas an always-wait rule fails to account for the first machine.

This example is the setting of the paper. In a flexible job shop scheduling problem, 
each operation must be assigned to one machine selected from a set of eligible machines, 
and the operations assigned to each machine must then be sequenced. When the shop uses automated
guided vehicles (AGVs), a third decision appears, namely which vehicle moves
the job between two machines. The three decisions are coupled, because a
machine that finishes early may wait for a vehicle and a vehicle that is busy
delays the next operation. In an energy-aware objective function, the coupling between scheduling decisions 
and the objective becomes tighter, because idle machines incur energy consumption even when 
they do not affect the makespan. The objective considered in this paper therefore integrates the makespan, 
the processing and idle energy consumption, and the loaded travel time.

The problem is challenging for three reasons. First, the assignment decisions are hard because 
a shorter loaded trip may require a longer empty trip. Second, machine sequencing is difficult because 
an earlier start on one machine can increase the idle gap on another. Third, vehicle sequencing 
must be coordinated with both assignment and machine sequencing. 
At each decision step, the number of joint actions is the product of the cardinalities of the candidate sets, 
and this number grows with the numbers of jobs, machines, and vehicles.
Because the three objective terms have different scales, the weights represent a trade-off that varies across planning scenarios.

Dispatching rules make the three coupled decisions in a single pass. They are computationally efficient and 
deterministic, but their solution quality depends heavily on the selected rule combination, 
and a fixed combination cannot adapt to the current shop state. In contrast, metaheuristics search the 
solution space more thoroughly and often return better schedules, at the expense of thousands of schedule 
evaluations per instance. Constraint programming and mixed-integer models give a bound or a strong
incumbent~\cite{yao2026cp}, but their run time grows quickly with the number of
operations and vehicles. Deep reinforcement
learning (DRL) offers a fourth option, a policy that constructs one schedule
in a single pass and can be reused across instances. Recent work applies DRL
to flexible job shops with AGVs~\cite{li2025tsmc,li2025plcsp,shi2025nested,
wang2025hierarchical}. Most of these controllers make the operation and
machine decisions and leave the vehicle to a rule, or solve the two layers
in sequence.

Three difficulties remain for a DRL controller in this setting. First, the
candidate sets change size at every step, because the number of eligible
machines depends on the operation and the number of available vehicles depends
on the workshop. An encoder must map a variable-size set to a fixed-size
representation without imposing an order. Second, the three decisions share
one return, and the environment gives no per-agent return. A critic for each
agent would have no target, so the contribution of one decision cannot be
separated from that of the others. Third, a top-level rule selector makes only
four to thirteen decisions per episode. The sample is small, and a
counterfactual baseline must generalize to actions that were not taken.

This paper studies our model, which responds to the three difficulties with a
shared architecture. Three actor networks make the operation, machine, and AGV
decisions. Each of the three shop agents encodes its own candidates with a
Set Transformer. The machine encoder groups its features into three views and
applies attention within a machine before it applies attention across machines. We train the
agents with a shared group-relative advantage and no value critic, and a
top-level policy selects a rule prior for each agent every eight low-level
steps, where each rule head receives the change of its own agent's cost. We
refer to our model as H-ST-GRPO.

Each design choice is driven by one of these difficulties. The Set Transformer’s permutation invariance 
allows the same weights to be used regardless of the number of operations or machines. 
Machine-internal grouping keeps the token count linear in the number of machines while enabling the model 
to compare the three feature views of a machine before comparing across machines. 
Group-relative advantage removes the critic, which offers the least target information here. 
A rule prior gives low-level agents a coarse action space to explore, and per-agent costs provide the top 
level with a signal unavailable from a shared return. Overall, this paper makes three contributions:


\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Problem and model.} We formulate a joint production and logistics
scheduling problem for a multi-workshop flexible job shop, in which each
workshop operates its own vehicle fleet and jobs are transferred between
workshops through load/unload stations. The operation, machine, and vehicle
decisions are integrated into a sequential decision-making process.
Specifically, the transport decision is generated at the same decision step as
the machine decision that triggers it. For a cross-workshop job, the same AGV
agent selects the source vehicle before the transfer and the target vehicle
after it.

\item \emph{Method.} We build a hierarchical multi-agent policy with four
agents. A top-level rule-prior selector periodically chooses one rule for each
shop agent and uses it as an action bias; each rule head receives the change of
its own local cost. The three shop agents produce the operation, machine, and
AGV decisions at the same step. Set Transformer encoders are used to handle
variable-size candidate sets, with one encoder assigned to each agent. The
machine encoder first compares features within a single machine and
subsequently across different machines. The shop agents are trained without a
critic and share a single group-relative advantage. Treating the weights as part
of the state input enables one policy to cover the entire weight surface.

\item \emph{Empirical findings.} The paper evaluates joint production and
logistics scheduling under a uniform protocol. The objective is lower than
rule-based dispatch and adapted DRL baselines at every size. For
decision-makers, genetic search offers greater advantages in smaller shops,
while the policy wins in larger shops and it can return a schedule in seconds.
\end{enumerate}

We compare our model with rule-based,
metaheuristic, and adapted DRL baselines on the same instances,
against the same objective and under the same transport model. We report the
objective and the run time of every method, the per-instance spread, and the
scope of the transport model. One schedule takes about 0.95~s per instance.
We also stop one machine for a repair in the middle of the schedule, to see
what a model that re-decides at every step does when a resource it has
already used becomes unavailable. The weights are an input of the policy, so we
also evaluate one policy at nine weight points without retraining it.
